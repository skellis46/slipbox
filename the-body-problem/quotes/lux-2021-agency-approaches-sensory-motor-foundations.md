# Agency approaches to embodiment: sensory and motor foundations

## Quotation

> The rise of embodiment concepts in the life sciences started out in the 1980s, when linguists, cognitive psychologist, and robotic engineers turned to the study of motor and sensory processing, emphasizing that they shape the way our mind works. Neuroscience and philosophy of mind contributed to further advance this perspective (Varela et al., 1991; Gibbs, 2005; Shapiro, 2011). Subsequently, a range of different concepts developed by building up on the joint idea that our bodily preconditions are the foundation for the perception of ourselves (in the sense of consciousness and interoception) and the perception of the environment with which we interact, affecting a range of processes and phenomena related to e.g., cognition, emotion processing, social interaction, aesthetic perception, and mental health. We subsume these as agency approaches to embodiment (see Table 1).

## Context

**Source-based paraphrase:** Lux and colleagues distinguish what they call **agency approaches**, concerned with the bodily preconditions and mobilisation of movement, action and psychological functions, from **environmental approaches**, which examine how environments and experiences become incorporated into bodily organisation over time. This passage summarises the historical emergence and subject matter of the former. The authors acknowledge that other ways of classifying embodiment approaches exist and that their discussion is selective.

## Relevance

**Project-wide:** Useful for documenting the origins and boundaries of Lux et al.'s integrative framework, which informed but does not dictate our maps. **Agency approaches** are Lux et al.'s grouping, not one of our seven provisional conceptions.

**LLM interpretation (provisional):** This passage connects perception of oneself and perception of the environment through bodily preconditions. It may help situate the project's discussions of sensory awareness and embodied practice, but does not establish either a definition of embodied sensitivity or degrees of embodiment.
