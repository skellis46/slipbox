# BT-06: Communicating bodily knowing

Status: **provisionally concluded – representational limitation, no map alteration**  
Agreed: 5 November 2026

## Question explored

Can the map accommodate different ways in which bodily knowing is communicated, including through propositional description, demonstration and guided participation?

This formulation developed from the question of performing versus describing movement (candidate C03), through consideration of *description* as one form of communication, and finally towards communication that facilitates another person's practical knowing. The question is a working formulation, not a claim that all communication conveys pre-existing knowledge.

## Bounded example: learning to knead dough

An experienced baker teaches a novice to knead dough by giving verbal instructions, demonstrating actions and guiding attention to variations in resistance, elasticity and texture as the learner handles the material.

These communicative forms can occur together. The example is **illustrative**, not a researched observation of a baker or evidence about the effectiveness of any teaching method. It was chosen as a non-dance example in response to SE's request to look outside dance.

## Primary conceptual account

Tim Ingold's account of the *education of attention* questions the treatment of learning as the transmission of representations from one individual to another (Ingold 2001). In *Making*, Ingold (2013) further develops an account in which teachers provide situations for learners to develop knowing through doing and attentive engagement rather than receive a ready-made stock of knowledge.

The point is **not** that speech is unimportant or that verbal instruction and participation are mutually exclusive. It is that communicating instructions and cultivating practical knowing are not necessarily the same process.

## Agreed provisional finding

The existing map can accommodate different practices through which bodily knowing is communicated, principally through relationality / sense-making and lived body / phenomenology. Ingold's (2001, 2013) account of the education of attention complicates models of communication understood solely as the transmission of pre-existing knowledge, drawing attention instead to the conditions through which practical understanding develops in situated activity.

The map can locate such processes but does not directly distinguish **communicating information** from **cultivating practical knowing through participation**. This is a **representational limitation**, not a demonstrated conceptual failure or grounds for changing the map.

## Implication for *The Body Problem*

The difficulty of communicating bodily knowing may lie partly in assuming that knowing must be detachable from the activity in which it develops in order to count as communicable knowledge. This is a possibility raised by the comparison, **not** a conclusion that bodily knowing is ineffable, that propositions are inadequate or that Ingold's account settles these matters.

The test therefore contributes both to evaluating the map and to the chapter's epistemological argument about how bodily knowing is described, recognised and valued.

## Boundaries and parked question

- **Communication is not identical to learning.** BT-06 considered how communication may help establish conditions for learning without attempting a general theory of learning.
- Communication is not guaranteed transmission of the expert's own experience or practical understanding.
- **Robotics, programming and machine learning:** SE raised whether teaching or communicating bodily capacities must be human-to-human. The discussion considered robot programming and learning from demonstration, then agreed this expanded the test too far and should be parked as a separate candidate. No claim about robot experience or knowledge was established.
- BT-06 did not settle what constitutes bodily knowing, the relation of knowing-how to knowing-that or the conditions for successful communication.
