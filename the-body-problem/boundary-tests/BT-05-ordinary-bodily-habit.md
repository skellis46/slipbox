# BT-05: Ordinary bodily habit

Status: **provisionally concluded – straightforward map fit**  
Discussed and agreed: 5 November 2026

## Question

Can the existing map adequately describe an ordinary bodily habit without requiring additional conceptual categories?

## Bounded example

Entering a familiar room in the dark and reaching for a light switch without explicitly calculating its position. This is an illustration generated for the discussion, not Merleau-Ponty's own example.

## Primary source

Merleau-Ponty, M. (1945/2012). *Phenomenology of Perception*. Translated by D. A. Landes. Routledge. See 'The Spatiality of One's Own Body and Motricity', especially the discussion of habit and examples of typing, organ playing and using a walking stick. His account treats bodily habit as practical familiarity with the world that is not reducible to either explicit intellectual knowledge or a mechanical series of reflexes. This is a source-based summary; the switch example is ours.

## Map application

- **Conceptions:** Strong fit with lived body / phenomenology. Bodily constraint / affordance is a plausible secondary location, an interpretation of the map rather than a claim by Merleau-Ponty.
- **Levels of investigation:** Sensorimotor organisation and action are relevant; a phenomenological account is not simply reducible to Lux et al.'s biological levels.
- **Knowledge modes:** Theoretical / conceptual work is central to this source. First-person and practice-based investigation are possible additional modes, not evidence supplied by the example.

## Agreed provisional finding

Ordinary bodily habit can be accommodated within the existing map, principally through lived body / phenomenology. Merleau-Ponty's account shows how practical bodily familiarity develops through repetition, learning and practice without requiring explicit knowledge of the spatial organisation of an action. The test demonstrates the map's descriptive usefulness more than its discriminatory power, and does not warrant a map change.

This again raises the role of training and practice in *developing* embodiment. However, developing an embodied capacity is not necessarily becoming *more embodied*, as distinguished in [BT-04](BT-04-degrees-of-embodiment.md).

## Deferred questions

- Distinguishing bodily knowing from being able to articulate or describe that knowing.
- Distinguishing **kinds of knowing** from the map's **evidence and knowledge modes**, which name how a phenomenon is investigated rather than necessarily what kind of knowledge is involved.

Both bear directly on the body problem. They are preserved in the [candidate pool](../future-tests/candidate-pool.md) rather than opened as active tests. The wider proposal that an ideal boundary test should both examine the map and develop the chapter's argument is recorded there as a selection preference.

## Limitation

Because lived body / phenomenology already appears on the map, this was a comparatively easy fit. Its limited discriminatory power is a finding about the selected test, not proof that habit is philosophically straightforward. No claim is made that the two parked questions have been resolved.
