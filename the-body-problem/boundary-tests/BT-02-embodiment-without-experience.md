**Summary** *(LLM (Claude), 26 November 2026)*: a passive dynamic walker is a simple machine that walks down a slope by itself, powered only by gravity, with no motor, brain or sensors. Some researchers call it embodied. What idea of embodiment allows that, and what does that idea leave out?

**Origin** *(SE, 26 November 2026)*: SE wanted to test the map against robots. A passive dynamic walker is about as "dumb" a robot as there is.

**Where it stands:** the map can include the walker under conception 2, bodily constraint / affordance (a body's physical shape and build shape what it can do). But Lux's seven levels, which run from genes to social life, do not really apply to a machine. The lesson recorded is that an example does not have to fit every part of the map: poor fits are worth recording, not hiding.

## Agreed question

> What conception of embodiment makes it possible to describe the passive walker as embodied, and what might that conception exclude?

## Case

Tad McGeer's passive dynamic walker shows how a suitably configured mechanical structure can produce a walking gait downhill through its interactions with gravity and the ground, without active central control. It is not navigating by selecting destinations or routes. Environmental interaction here means physical coupling with slope and terrain, not intentional navigation.

Lux et al. (2021) explicitly discuss passive dynamic walking in their account of embodied robotics. So the case is not an accidental addition from outside the literature behind the map.

## Why this case matters

SE and LLM reached a shared working position: **some conceptions of embodiment do not require a living or experiencing body**. This is not a claim that all theories of embodiment accept nonliving systems. It means that life or first-person experience cannot be assumed to be necessary conditions for every conception represented on the map.

The implications for dance and somatics are potentially significant but remain unexplored. Neither experience nor embodied knowing has been declared irrelevant to those fields.

## Attempt at locating the case

- **Conception:** conception 2, bodily constraint / affordance, appears promising, because mechanical organisation and environmental interaction contribute to action.
- **Lux level:** level 4, sensory and motor activity, is the nearest, but the walker moves without sensory processing or control in the usual robotic sense.
- **Evidence / knowledge mode:** "behavioural / experimental measurement" and "theoretical or conceptual work" may be relevant; these labels have not been checked against the actual study.
- **Disciplinary colour:** the map's legend colours each source by discipline. Robotics has no colour of its own.

These are preliminary placements. They do not establish either a successful fit or a boundary.

## Arguments discarded or qualified

These were suggested and then dropped or qualified during the exchange between SE and LLM.

- A simple pendulum was suggested as a counterexample and rejected as too weak a comparison: it does not walk through contact with terrain. It should not be used as a decisive comparison.
- The suggestion that inclusion in the map should depend on what an account "contributes to understanding" was withdrawn as an inadequate criterion.
- Environmental interaction is not the same as conscious perception or intentional navigation.
- Lux et al.'s treatment of embodied robotics does not supply a single universal definition of embodiment.

## Methodological reflection: somatic standpoint

SE's experience of embodiment comes especially through somatic practice, sensitivity to bodily sensation and something akin to *felt sense*. This is a situated starting point and a methodological limit: it can incline the enquiry towards awareness without warranting awareness as a condition of every conception of embodiment.

A working distinction from the exchange between SE and LLM is between **bodily organisation taking part in producing behaviour** and an account of **awareness**. The first is the focus of BT-02. The second remains open, potentially as a separate boundary test (it later became [BT-03](BT-03-awareness-and-bodily-responsiveness.md)).

For the passive walker, three things are distinguished:
- **Physical coupling:** slope, gravity and ground contact take part in producing its movement.
- **Sensitivity or responsiveness:** its behaviour depends on these conditions, but that does not by itself mean it senses, discriminates or represents anything.
- **Awareness:** a possibly experiential notion which the walking mechanism's behaviour does not establish. Awareness should not be treated as the same as consciousness until the term is clarified.

The proposed distinction between **being aware of** an environment and **accounting for** an environment needs care. McGeer's walker has no internal model of its environment; its behaviour is physically conditioned by it. This is neither evidence of felt awareness nor necessarily a case of the walker explicitly accounting for its surroundings.

A possible further question, *not an agreed or numbered test*: what would make environmental sensitivity count as awareness, and which conceptions of embodiment presuppose or reject that move?

## Tension to preserve

The map should be permissive enough to trouble normal assumptions about embodiment. Yet if everything counts as embodied, the map can no longer tell anything apart. This tension is unresolved, and is part of what BT-02 set out to investigate.

## Agreed provisional outcome (SE and LLM)

The passive walker can be accommodated within conception 2, **bodily constraint / affordance**. However, its relationship to Lux's seven levels is uncertain. This suggests a possible limitation in how the map's dimensions are combined, rather than a failure to recognise the example as embodied.

The agreement concerns this **provisional assessment**, not the resolution of every methodological question the test raised.

## Agreed principle: failure points (SE and LLM, 8 October 2026)

An account **does not have to fit every dimension** of the map. Poor fits, or "failure points", are recorded rather than smoothed away. They may reveal a limit of the map, a difference in scope between its dimensions or something about the conception of embodiment under examination. A dimension that simply does not apply is different from a placement that misrepresents the account.

In this case:
- **Conceptions:** bodily constraint / affordance is a productive fit. The case troubles any tacit expectation that embodiment must involve a living or experiencing body.
- **Lux's levels:** level 4, sensory and motor activity, is at best an imperfect analogy. The walker moves without the kind of sensory system the label implies. This is a **poor fit or possible non-applicability**, not grounds to force a classification.
- **Evidence / knowledge modes:** experimental and theoretical investigation are broadly accommodated, although the labels may not capture the particular relation between mechanical design and demonstration. This has not been examined in depth.

The failure point concerning Lux's levels is itself useful: the map combines dimensions developed with different assumptions and scopes. Their uneven fit may teach people something about the conceptions of embodiment the map brings together.

## Remaining limits

No map categories have been changed. The analysis does not establish that the walker is aware of its environment. The more general question of what the map ultimately excludes is still open. BT-02 may be revisited.
