Research on nature is often shaped by instrumental questions about benefit, for example whether time outdoors reduces stress, improves mood or supports resilience. While important, such approaches can overlook aspects of experience that are difficult to measure yet culturally and intellectually significant. In sustained practices of movement, attention and attunement outdoors, people may encounter shifts in self-experience, bodily sensing and relation to place, including experiences of permeability, altered self-boundary and heightened awareness of more-than-human life. These experiences sit uneasily within dominant wellbeing frameworks and remain underexplored in current research. This project asks how arts and humanities led, first-person and practice-based methods can bring such experiences into clearer view. It also examines whether in situ sampling can be brought into productive dialogue with phenomenological description without reducing complex lived experience to thin categories. In doing so, the project repositions nature experience as a question of relation, method and knowledge, not only outcome.


- How do sustained, nature based embodied practices reshape experiences of self, bodily sensing, attention and environmental attunement? 
- What kinds of nature experience are obscured or flattened when research is organised primarily around instrumental wellbeing outcomes? 
- How can first person and practice based methods generate richer accounts of subtle, relational and more than human dimensions of outdoor experience? 
- How might in situ experience sampling be brought into productive dialogue with phenomenological description without reducing complex lived experience to thin data? 
- What new cultural and epistemological account of nature experience emerges when embodied relation, rather than measurable outcome, becomes the starting point?

Much research on nature begins with the question of benefit: does time outdoors reduce stress, improve mood or build resilience? Those questions matter, but they do not always leave much room for experiences that are harder to measure and harder to describe. In sustained practices of movement, attention and attunement outdoors, people sometimes report a loosening of self, changes in bodily feeling, a different relation to place and a heightened sense of more-than-human presence. These are not just wellbeing outcomes. They also raise questions about perception, relation and what counts as knowledge in nature research. This project explores how arts and humanities led, first-person and practice-based methods might attend to such experiences more carefully. It also asks whether in situ sampling can sit alongside phenomenological description without forcing subtle and unstable experiences into neat categories. The aim is to make room for a fuller account of nature experience.
