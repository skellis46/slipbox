# Productivity Field Guide 2026 by David Sparks

To truly transform your life, you must stop solving for speed and start solving for meaningfulness.


Everyone has the best of intentions when they ask for just one more little thing, but every “yes” to the unimportant is a “no” to your Arete.


For every role you value, ask yourself: what does a shitty version of me look like in this role?


During your Daily Journaling or Weekly Review, don’t just list your wins. Look for the fingerprints of the shadow. Acknowledging the shadow is what allows you to move forward. You cannot find tranquility if you are constantly surprised by your own bad behavior.


Before you can effectively use hyper-scheduling or project management tools, you must recognize the “sleeping version” of yourself that casually accepts new obligations without thought. We say yes to a community board, a new client, or a social commitment because we don’t want to let the tribe down, or because the request strokes our ego.  Each yes represents another commitment that pulls you away from your Arete.


In meditation, the goal is to create a gap between a stimulus and your reaction. The Arete Radar applies this principle to commitment management.  When a new obligation appears, whether a request to speak at a conference or an invitation to join a committee, the radar triggers a pause. Instead of an immediate, reflexive yes, you create a moment to stop and think.  This gap is where your intentionality lives.  It’s the difference between being awake and sleeping through your life. In that pause, you ask one question: does this move me toward my Arete or away from it?


It requires courage to take a stand on what matters to you. It means acknowledging that you cannot be everything to everyone, and that by saying no to the unimportant, you’re saying yes to your own excellence.


During your reviews, ask questions like: ​​Am I dedicating sufficient time and energy to each role? ​​Are there any roles I’m consistently neglecting? ​​What factors are contributing to the imbalance? ​​What adjustments can I make to my schedule or habits to create more balance?


The only legitimate measuring stick in this system is who you were when you started. Success is measured by looking backward at your own transformation.  When you conduct a quarterly review, don’t ask “Am I perfect yet?” Ask instead: “How much progress have I made since the person who wrote these words three months ago?”


When you look back at your reviews from a year ago, you will often find you were struggling with problems that are now completely solved. That is the evidence you need.


What I didn’t realize, however, was how useful habits can be as tools to find your Arete in roles that don’t lend themselves to projects.


The trick is in recognizing that habits are just as important as projects in getting to your ideal you. For every role, you should be actively considering projects and habits that can help you make progress.


During every Quarterly Review, perform an Overhead Audit. List every active project and estimate the management time it generates. If your Manager self is spending more time managing projects than your Maker self is spending doing them, your system is broken.


For me and the roles that I pursue, I spend a lot more time making than managing, but that is how it should be. I track my time during the day, and I assign the maker, manager, and consumer tags to many of the things I do. Then I can go back and look at those tags to see how the balance is actually working out in my life.

From the first eye drop to the moment I start making is about an hour. At no point during that hour have I checked email or the news. My mornings are in isolation from the rest of the world.


Just say to yourself, “Sure, I’ll go out and play in the woodshop, but first I’m going to give this just five minutes.” In that, I’m giving myself permission to escape the block, guilt-free. I just have to first give the block five minutes. Nine out of ten times that five minutes turns into an hour or more.


I look at my fluid blocks and then schedule them into specific time slots based on my energy, available time, and current priorities. If something doesn’t fit or feel right, I can easily move it to another day. It’s hyper scheduling, but with the flexibility to make final decisions on the day of execution rather than locking everything in advance.


To ensure your compass bearing remains accurate and deeply internalized, after you’ve spent some time working on your Arete, (perhaps a year), I recommend the blank page ritual. Instead of reviewing or lightly editing your existing Arete statements, rewrite them entirely from scratch.


The process is simple but demanding. Do not open your previous Arete document. Open a blank document or pull out a blank piece of paper. Write down each of your roles one by one. For each role, ask: is this role still appropriate for my life today?  For every role that remains, write out your Arete definition in the first person. Describe how you act, how you feel, and how you interact with the world when you are your best self.  Only after you have written your new definitions should you open your previous Arete statement. Compare the two. You will often find that you used different language or focused on new nuances. These differences are the evidence of your evolution.


A secondary benefit of rewriting from scratch is that it acknowledges the fluid nature of life. You are not a static entity. Your priorities in your 30s are not the same as those in your 50s. Roles emerge and roles fade.  If you simply edit an old document, you are likely to carry over ghost values that no longer resonate with your current season of life. Rewriting from memory ensures that only the values that are truly alive within you make it onto the page.  While you can perform this ritual in a blank digital document, there is distinct power in handwriting your Arete. Handwriting is significantly slower than typing, which provides the necessary gap time for deeper contemplation. Many find that using a high-quality notebook or a special pen turns the process into a meaningful ritual rather than just another task to check off.


Don’t see your problems as enemies to be defeated. See them as friends offering you an opportunity. Every problem you face is a chance to exercise your Arete. The difficult client is an opportunity to practice patience and communication. The technical challenge is an opportunity to deepen your expertise. The family conflict is an opportunity to become a better listener.


The next time a problem appears, catch yourself before the automatic dread response kicks in. Instead of “Oh no! a problem!” try “Oh yes! a problem!” What does this problem require of me? What skill does it demand I develop? What part of my Arete does it challenge me to embody?
