# Heffernan - Beyond Determinism and Submission

_previous note:_ [[Odell - Saving Time]]

> We have all grown up in an age less amusing than Gilbreth’s but just as obsessed by efficiency—bigger, faster, cheaper, with order, automation and machines. Which is why, towards the end of her typically insightful book, *We are not Machines,* the FT journalist Sarah O’Connor poses a great question. How have we come to think of *ourselves* as machines? Why do we submit to being managed *like* machines, even in some cases already, *by* machines? Why and how have we absorbed and accepted machine-like expectations of ourselves: programmed to be efficient economic self-maximizers, grading our employees, bosses, partners and friends? Efficiently measuring our steps, our calories, skin temperature, heart rate, the hours and quality of our sleep? Festooned with monitors, our jewellery now gauges blood pressure while gadgets protrude from our ears isolating us in a personalized soundtrack of our lives, spending more time with new tech than new neighbours?

> Management thinkers found much of value in Skinner. Rewards and punishments, rigid definitions and goals could be used to maximize efficiency and give managers a powerful sense of control. In most companies today, success is defined by the whole kit and kaboodle of performance management: detailed job descriptions, Key Performance Indicators, targets, timetables, rankings. All of these give managers great certainty, of a kind they crave: units of measurement assuring them they were doing a good job. The blowback, to some of us, became quickly apparent: manage people like robots and that is exactly how they will behave. Any creativity disappears in the mad dash for efficiency and rewards. Tell people to compete to keep the job, get the bonus, and they do—especially when a lot is at stake.

> Less obvious was a slower side-effect: For the sake of standardized measurement, work must be marked against a standard answer, so the easiest, most convenient pathway to the reward is to become not a Thinker but an expert second-guesser. Which goes a long way towards explaining the grievances of CEOs I work with, who all complain that they hire ostensibly well-educated graduates, highly credentialed, whom they find cannot think for themselves. Of course they can’t; that isn’t what they were taught and it isn’t what the system rewards.

> The problem with Skinner isn’t that he was wrong. He was right. You can modify behaviour with rewards. Standardizing work means you can measure everything and earn your unambiguous prize.

> It’s hard to be told we’ve been taken for a ride; the easier, more comfortable response is the slightly cynical shrug—yes we know we are being manipulated, but what can we do? Seduced by convenience, laziness, and a carefully curated addiction to consumption, it can feel a whole lot simpler to concede our stupidity and buy some more comfort. Learned helplessness: A little less freedom, a little less dignity; the machine is smarter than we are and it never sleeps. We’ve had 20 years of Skinnerian conditioning … and it’s working.

> Or as the University of Pennsylvania psychologist Angela Duckworth once exclaimed excitedly to me “We’ve seen the enemy—and it’s us. AND we are going to fix it!” Even though we never asked to be fixed.

> No wonder we feel so lonely. Sucked into a false ideal, that the efficient, convenient, effortless life was the ultimate reward. But human existence isn’t a problem to be solved. The compulsion to reduce everything we are, or do or want, to standardized, measurable, frictionless modules of predictable experience doesn’t enhance but shrinks us, leaving us predictable, manageable but hardly alive at all.

> It isn’t just that our convenience devices make other people in our lives superfluous—we needn’t talk to a shopkeeper, ask a stranger for directions or a friend for advice. We don’t even need to stay in touch with our own physical bodies: our gadgets do that for us. I mentored a CEO who, in a meeting, realized he was having a heart attack only when his Apple watch told him so. Just data points in a cloud, we are in danger fulfilling Max Frisch’s definition of technology: the art of arranging the world so that we don’t have to experience it.

> How do we defy this fate? The critic George Steiner once said that language is the main instrument of our refusal to accept the world as it is. I would broaden that to say that it is art that is the main, the most ancient and fundamental, instrument of our refusal to accept the world as it is. It is in our intrinsic creativity—the creativity we are all born with and that, active or latent, remains within us. Frequently mocked, trivialized or marginalized, it remains our defining, enduring human capacity.

> Embracing it begins in such an unobtrusive way: by stepping out, into the world without an agenda—no goal—except to look. Wander. Wonder. Not solving a problem but being receptive. It’s what the anthropologist James Clifford called ‘deep hanging out.’ Being curious and paying attention: to the banal, the random, the inexplicable and the unintentional. It looks like doing nothing but, as the director Mike Leigh told me, “nothing happening is something happening”—if you take the time to consider it.
