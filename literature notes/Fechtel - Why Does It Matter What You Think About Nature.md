# Fechtel - Why Does It Matter What You Think About Nature

_previous note:_ [[Kimmerer - Serviceberry Abundance and Reciprocity]]

> We, too, are natural beings. The very biology of our bodies is fundamental to our humanness. Our bodies enact in miniature the rhythms of the world outside our hermetic buildings: cycles of heat and cold; of air, water, fuel, and waste; of activity and rest; of growth and decay; of fecundity and barrenness. If we are indifferent to or antagonistic to the rhythms of our own bodies, we can feel the effects. That the body is not in all parts and times picturesque, inoffensive to the senses, cultivated, manicured, and sweet-smelling is no knock upon it, nor is it any reason to disdain the body categorically.

> The body requires an attitude, however covert or known by other means, toward nature, and not just our own. We are not self-contained, but each day need food and drink; each hour need movement; each moment need breath. Survival is a refreshingly immediate drive. It makes nature both an **adversary** and a **resource**.

> Our attitude toward nature is important. First, it is a source of moral formation. For example, maximizing the extraction of natural resources for immediate gain degrades the individual’s sense of care for the flourishing of others and of future generations by making his chief aim and goal extend no further than his own body
