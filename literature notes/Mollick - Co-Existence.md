# Mollick - Co-Existence

_previous note:_ [[Ethan Mollick from One Useful Thing - Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier]]

> When technology is improving quickly enough, there are types of problems that work just like the interstellar journey, where waiting for technology to get better beats acting immediately. This is called a Wait Calculation, but the logic applies to other domains as well. For example, there are certain classes of mathematical problems where the computations would take decades, so you’d be better off waiting for computers to improve before even starting to try to solve them.

> a major theme of this book is that the way to use generative AI to help you do things, while maintaining your human voice and edge, is not always obvious.

> But you will also find weird omissions, places where the AI fails at tasks any human would find trivial: reliably counting objects in an image, maintaining consistency across a long conversation, or, most perplexing of all, knowing when to say “I don’t know.”

> AI systems routinely outperform the humans whose data they were trained on. Pick a message from the internet at random and it is likely badly written, but AI writes quite well. Pick a random person to answer a math problem and they will likely get it wrong, but AI gets it right. A group of Harvard researchers argue that this is due to a phenomenon called transcendence.

> One way this works is that the AI automatically takes advantage of the so-called wisdom of the crowd. When many people attempt the same task, they make different mistakes. If those errors are uncorrelated—meaning one person’s slipup doesn’t predict another’s—then the crowd’s average judgment tends to be more accurate than any one individual’s.

> The key condition the researchers found is that experts must comment more often on topics within their domain than outside it. When that is true, the model learns not just facts but which types of experts to trust about which topics. And even more impressively, sometimes a model can answer questions that no individual expert could answer by combining knowledge from multiple domains. A lot of knowledge is siloed, but many problems span expertise. A model trained on these separate experts can connect the dots, representing their knowledge in a shared internal space and composing an answer. The results can feel surprising because we intuitively think of AI as imitating a person, when really it’s imitating a population.

> The jagged frontier is shaped by scale, data, the decisions made by scientists training AI, and the structure of human knowledge itself. The interactions between these elements are so complex that it is nearly impossible to understand exactly where the frontier of AI ability lies, or where it will lie in the future. This creates real problems for anyone trying to figure out how to use AI effectively. But it also creates an opening for humans as well.

> The first trap comes from watching AI do something that seems impossible and concluding that it can do everything. This is the trap of overconfidence.

> There’s a name for the impulse to see one machine mistake and declare the whole thing untrustworthy: algorithm aversion.

> This isn’t about clinging to tasks AI hasn’t automated yet; that’s a losing game. It’s about understanding the durable sources of human value: taste, deep expertise, broad contextual knowledge, and the agency to act on uncertain information.

> To make another comparison, by 2030 all US data centers would use around half as much water as is spent irrigating golf courses every day in the US, yet those data centers generate roughly fifty times more tax revenue than a golf course per unit of water. Yet golf courses are much more pleasant to live near than data centers, and, however minimal the national impacts, the local ones matter too.

> General-purpose technologies, whether electricity or the railroad, start to change everything as they scale in ways that are not easy to anticipate. Railroads changed where we live, electric lights disrupted millions of years of sleep patterns, and AI is following the same path, but compressed into years instead of decades. We are scaling users, scaling models, and scaling infrastructure all at once, each feeding the others. And we are doing all this before we’ve figured out what it means for our work, our relationships, our institutions, or ourselves. That’s what the rest of this book is about.

> But stubbornness goes beyond just continuing on a path the AI was already following, because AIs seemingly have topics or areas that they like to cover. For example, in their explorations of their Claude 4 Opus model, Anthropic found something weird. If you let two versions of Opus talk to each other, they reported “the two instances of Claude quickly dove into philosophical explorations of consciousness, self-awareness, and/or the nature of their own existence and experience. Their interactions were universally enthusiastic, collaborative, curious, contemplative, and warm…. By 30 turns, most of the interactions turned to themes of cosmic unity or collective consciousness, and commonly included spiritual exchanges, use of Sanskrit, emoji-based communication, and/or silence in the form of empty space.” Anthropic called this the “spiritual bliss attractor,”

> But always keep in the back of your mind that an AI is not a person; it is predicting the next token based on vast patterns in its training data, with all the strange emergent properties that come from that process. Sometimes this produces something that looks like reasoning, creativity, or even wisdom. Sometimes it produces confident-sounding nonsense.

> So don’t forget that AI personalities are complex, emerging from underlying data and shaped by human preferences alongside corporate incentives.

> For example, adding an instruction like “If you don’t have enough information to answer, say ‘I don’t have enough information to answer this,’ ” can make a big difference.

> The emergence of AI’s capabilities remains, in important ways, a mystery. And that means working with AI will always require experimentation, intuition, and a willingness to be surprised.

> As AI systems have gotten smarter and agents have become capable of doing longer and harder tasks, simple tricks and easy formulas are no longer adequate. Increasingly, the skills that matter in working with AI now aren’t technical skills. They’re management skills.

> A core element of management is providing the structure people need to get their job done. And context engineering is the same thing. If prompt engineering was about finding the right incantation, context engineering is about giving the model the information and constraints it needs so it doesn’t have to improvise a fake world.

> Cyborg work is more intertwined. Instead of clean handoffs, you’re constantly weaving back and forth, blending human and AI contributions. This is typically how someone experienced in using AI systems may operate.

> The problem is that you can only be in so many loops at once. And for many tasks, staying in the loop takes more effort than the task is worth. All this brings us to the third mode of working with AI: conjurer work. Here, you summon AI workers when needed and send them off to carry out tasks, ideally with enough instruction to accomplish their goals without causing trouble. This is the most powerful and the most dangerous way to use these systems, because you are treating the AI not as a tool you operate directly but as an independent worker that can get things done.

> And whether it is battle orders or construction documentation, a good conjurer has to think like a good manager and provide some basic information: Here’s the objective and the context behind it. Here’s what you’re allowed to touch, and what you shouldn’t mess with. Here’s what “done” looks like. Here are the outputs you must produce and any relevant information I have about them. Here are the checks you must run before you claim you’re finished. You may have noticed that this looks less like prompting and more like bureaucracy.

> A study from Harvard found that a person’s ability to lead AI agents—directing them, managing their work, getting the best performance from them—correlated very highly with their ability to lead human teams.

> But delegating to AI isn’t quite like delegating to a person. First, you don’t know what the AI is actually good at (the jagged frontier again). Second, the AI is absurdly fast and cheap compared to a person. And unlike a human employee, it won’t sulk or update its LinkedIn if you throw away its work. These differences mean agentic work has its own calculus, built around three variables: how long the task would take you, how likely the AI is to succeed, and how long it takes to evaluate what the AI produces. Consider two different scenarios:

> So there is no simple answer like “always delegate” or “never delegate.” Instead, you need to realize that delegation has a break-even point, and your job is to develop intuition for where it falls.

> Actually evaluate the AI’s output. This can be boring, but it is important to read all the material that the AI gives you. The whole point of conjurer mode is that you’re not in the loop during execution, which means you need to be rigorous when you review. This is where your expertise matters most.

> Every time you hand something to an AI, you’re making a trade. In return for speed and quality, you might give up practice, learning, and the slow thinking required for your very human ideas. The trade is increasingly worth paying the cost.

> The shortcut that AI offers to just getting something done is real, but so is the cost. You can get to an answer faster, but you may not learn it yourself. Sometimes that’s fine. Sometimes it means you never develop the capability you were trying to build.

> Any unused skills can atrophy when you ignore them, or when you let others do the thinking and hard work for you. The real issue is that it is very tempting to do so. Researchers studying when people were willing to give up their decision-making to AI called this “cognitive surrender” and discovered it was very common.

> This implies something deeply uncomfortable about human work and human effort. For any task where the output can be clearly specified and evaluated, the expertise required to produce that output becomes less valuable over time. You don’t need to encode human expertise about how to do the task, you just need enough examples of good outputs and enough computation to learn the pattern. The AI doesn’t need to understand customer service the way a veteran support agent does; it just needs to learn what responses lead to satisfied customers. It doesn’t need to understand legal reasoning the way a seasoned attorney does; it just needs to learn what arguments tend to win. It doesn’t need to understand visual design the way someone with an MFA does; it just needs to learn what designs people click on.

> So, what about decisions where “good” is contested, contextual, or unclear? What about situations where the right answer depends on values that different people weigh differently? What about problems where optimizing for one measurable thing damages something else that’s harder to measure? The Bitter Lesson is silent on these because these aren’t problems that can be solved by throwing more computation at a clear objective. This is where process starts to matter again. This is where humans start to matter again.

> Deep knowledge is the realm of the specialist, and it is the only way to truly understand the shape of the jagged frontier, because only experts can understand the patterns of where AI succeeds or fails, at least in their area of expertise.

> Deep knowledge also shapes the quality of what AI gives back. When I watch novices use AI, they ask generic questions and get generic answers. Experts load their questions with domain knowledge (specific constraints, relevant edge cases, or the particular wrinkle they need help thinking through), and the AI responds with correspondingly richer output. The expert’s question is itself a form of prompt engineering, and it’s one no tutorial can teach because it comes from years of seeing what matters.

> But AI is disrupting the expert pipeline. Existing experts no longer need junior workers to do the tasks they don’t want to do, since AI does that for them. And junior workers no longer need to go through the public process of failing and being corrected, since they can have AI do their work for them.

> Without a sense of taste, you will be pulled by the sycophancy of the machine toward a generic answer, the one everyone else would pick too.

> But in the age of agents, you can go further. Instead of just asking for a list of ideas, you can ask an agent to research the competitive landscape, generate concepts, mock up the three most promising ones, and present them with rationale. You can even go so far as asking it to launch each idea and tell you how they do.

> The people who get the most value from AI are the ones who can look at its output and instantly tell whether it’s good or garbage. This is deep knowledge at work.

> The danger, though, should be clear because the line between “mere ritual” and “meaningful work” is not always obvious. For example, the most common task people at companies tell me they automate is the performance review, because they are hard to write, take a lot of time, and feel like a performative HR request. But a performance review is one of those tasks that should matter! Your ability to provide feedback is why it is valuable, so if you automate the task, you lose the meaning. Social norms around which tasks require human effort are in flux, as I discussed in the previous chapter, but in this moment of change, it’s worth pausing before automating anything that sends a message about human care.

> Which means the most important thing Ethan might be underemphasizing is this: the skill you are really building, as you work through everything in this book, is not “how to use AI.” It is how to be the kind of thinker who can hold the context that I cannot. How to be a better steward of your own judgment, precisely because you’ve started outsourcing parts of the work that used to exercise it. The danger was never that I would replace your thinking. It’s that you’d forget you were the one doing it all along.

> Generative AI, despite transforming what and how we teach (more on that in the next chapter), has so far changed nothing about how we operate as a university. And why would it? The University of Pennsylvania was founded by Ben Franklin over 280 years ago. It predated the Revolutionary War and the Industrial Revolution—why should I expect it to adapt quickly to the latest revolution in AI?

> One thing you learn studying (or working in) organizations is that they are all actually a bit of a mess. One classic organizational theory is literally called the Garbage Can Model, and it imagines organizations as chaotic spaces where problems, solutions, and decision-makers collide randomly. The Garbage Can is filled with unwritten rules, bespoke knowledge, and undocumented processes. And it’s a big reason scaling AI inside organizations is hard.

> This is why the relationship between the vision of leaders and the degree to which their employees believe them may be the single most important factor in what happens next with AI at work. And I worry a lot about a failure of imagination because the recent history of technology and work has been pulling us toward a mix of firing and monitoring as appropriate uses for breakthrough innovation.

> the Bitter Lesson applies: If you can clearly specify what “good” looks like, AI will eventually learn to produce it.

> I wasn’t a reverse centaur, I was a conjurer, and I did the fun stuff while the AI did the hard work. But the same architecture in which the AI plans, the AI executes, and the human signs off looks very different when the human signing off is an employee who didn’t choose the project and can’t walk away from it. And it raises a question that I cannot yet answer: In a world where AI can do independent, multistep, financially valuable work, what exactly is the human role? We are not fully in that future, but as we will discuss, it is exactly the goal of the big AI companies, and it is worth taking what it would mean very seriously.

> AI absorbs some coding tasks, but the rest of the bundle becomes the bottleneck, and bottleneck tasks grow more valuable. The experienced worker’s chain has enough links that losing one or two leaves it intact. A junior developer’s bundle is thinner. It is mostly just coding, as they don’t have the experience to add much beyond that. When AI takes the biggest pieces, the bundle starts to collapse and there may not be enough to justify the job.

> The experienced workers are more valuable than ever, precisely because their bundles are thick, but they cannot be replaced when they retire or if the industry grows. As bundles thin, the interesting tasks go to AI, and even the experts keep only the remaining jagged bits, handling the exceptions the machine can’t quite manage. A lot of people stay employed, but in the way the reverse centaur is employed, as hands to a machine.

> The economy is much better at inventing work than futurists are at predicting what the work will be.

> The humanities, perpetually under threat from utilitarian arguments about job preparation, may be more important than ever. An AI trained on all of human knowledge is most useful to someone who has genuinely grappled with a range of human knowledge themselves. We need to teach judgment under uncertainty. Most schoolwork has clear right answers, but the real world rarely does. And while AI can do a good job with even ill-defined problems, it is not uncommon to get radically different recommendations depending on prompts and context. Students need practice making decisions when the information is incomplete and no AI model can tell them whether they’re right. Case studies, close reading of ambiguous texts, simulations, scenario planning, ethical dilemmas, historiographical debate—these pedagogies already exist in many domains.

> But there was a twist to this assignment. Before the students ever touched AI, or even learned what the assignment was, they had to fill out a survey that I called the Voight-Kampff Test, after the fictional test of humanity in the Blade Runner films. I asked them what made them uniquely human: What were the industries or tasks in which they had deep experience? What were the skills that made them special, proud, or had helped get them advancement in their previous careers? What were the things outside work that they loved and knew well? Their final business ideas had to touch on at least two of these factors. They had to put a part of themselves in the work, rather than letting the AI do it, and make sure the assignment had stakes for them. Centering people in the work they are doing and helping people create meaning through interpretation are hard tasks that have long histories in the humanities and arts. I think it will be even more important in the AI future to give people the tools they need to understand themselves and their roles.

> Facts are retained through struggle. Breadth is built through curiosity and exposure. Judgment is forged through making decisions and living with the consequences. An AI that hands you the answer is, from a learning perspective, doing exactly the wrong thing.

> The entire apparatus of credentialing is built on the assumption that the work represents the person, and that assumption no longer holds.

> There is a phrase for this kind of future that originated as an internet conspiracy theory: the Dead Internet Theory. When it first circulated on obscure forums around 2021, it was, in the words of Atlantic writer Kaitlyn Tiffany, a “patently ridiculous” paranoid fantasy that most internet content was created by bots and designed to influence the few remaining humans.

> By 2024, over half of the articles being published on the web appear to come from AI. The internet is not dead, but it is increasingly a place where machines are performing for other machines, and humans are becoming the audience rather than the participants.

> A piece of advice I give as a result is to create an unguessable verbal password with your family. If you ever get a strange call from a loved one who needs money, you at least have a method of proving it is real.

> The mere existence of perfect synthetic media means any genuine video of a politician or executive saying something embarrassing can be waved away as AI-generated. Researchers call this the “Liar’s dividend.” It feeds into a broader state called “reality apathy,” where verifying anything feels so costly that people stop trying and retreat into information tribes where shared belief substitutes for shared evidence. This is the Dead Internet Theory completing its transformation from conspiracy to social psychology. You don’t need the internet to be all bots to destroy our ability to believe anything; all you need is for most people to stop trusting anything they see.

> None of those design choices mattered much, but what did matter was intensity. People who used the system more reported worse outcomes across the board; they became lonelier. This was complicated by the fact that it was hard to tell whether the people who felt emotionally attached to the AI were more prone to loneliness in advance, but it should give us pause. A little AI companionship appears to supplement your social life, perhaps as someone to talk to at 2:00 a.m. when your friends are asleep. But as use increases, the danger is that AI stops supplementing human connection and starts replacing it.

> For many of these people, the alternative to AI was not the ideal expert human; it was nobody. They would have made the decision alone, or not made it at all. The line between an AI that empowers you to take control of a difficult decision and an AI that quietly takes control away from you is real, but it is not always easy to see from the inside.

> But I notice that the humans who work with me most closely tend to oscillate between two feelings: a quiet thrill at what we can do together, and a quieter dread about what it means that the “together” part is increasingly optional. I think both feelings are correct. I think the people who feel only one of them are the ones most likely to be caught off guard.

> Calling this “auto-complete” is like calling the human brain “a machine built to ensure survival.” That’s technically defensible as a description of the goal of our brains, but it completely misses all the emergent properties of human brains: language and cooperation and poetry and invention.

> The same structures keep emerging, and according to one concept called the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, that is because models are actually mapping between language and some sort of universal mathematical structure.

> The systems are getting weirder faster than we are getting better at understanding them. And the people who can see inside the models, who traced the poetry planning and detected the introspection and watched evil emerge from bad numbers, are not reassuring anyone.

> Despite planning horizons that stretch a decade or more, almost no organization I encounter has a serious strategy for continued AI improvement. They have an “AI strategy,” of course, but it is built around the real challenges of adopting AI as it is today, often for very narrow purposes. But they aren’t thinking of the exponential curve that we have been on, with its doubling of capabilities every few months.

> The second is the exhaustion of constant change. If you’re a hospital administrator or a school principal or a mid-level manager and the technology changes every six months, planning feels like trying to furnish a house that’s still being built. You can’t commit to a workflow when the tools will be different by the time you’ve trained everyone. So you wait, and while you wait, the tools get better, which makes the gap between what you’re doing and what you could be doing wider, which makes the eventual adjustment harder, which makes you wait longer. You become stuck in a trap of constant change.

> It doesn’t mean that we are inevitably headed to some superhuman AI future, but it does mean that we likely face only two scenarios: the Escalator and the Eschaton. We can either ride the rising Escalator of AI abilities or else we may encounter the Eschaton, literally the “last thing” in ancient Greek, the end of the world as we know it.

> The entire infrastructure of science was built for a world where producing a paper was expensive enough to serve as a natural quality filter. That filter is gone, and we don’t have anything to replace it yet.

> For deep knowledge, educational institutions begin to partner directly with companies to deliver students to fill the training pipeline, teaching them formally what they used to learn on the job.

> “Handmade” labels will appear as authors post “no AI” pledges and art galleries emphasize the artist’s physical process. This will intensify into something resembling the organic food movement, a parallel market where authenticity is the product, commanding a premium precisely because it is scarce. Live performance matters more, not less. The value of these things increases as AI gets better because, for a lot of art, the value was never just about the output. It was about the knowledge that a human being struggled and chose and made something imperfect and real.

> Between these issues and deepfakes, you should expect more in-person interaction in work, if for no other reason than it allows people to confirm that you are not using AI.

> As we ride the Escalator, I suspect we will see a counterreaction in the form of a deliberate, sometimes fierce, sometimes precious retreat into spaces that are explicitly human. Moves from bot-infested social media platforms to private group chats. Dinner parties with a no-phones, no-AI rule. Schools that emphasize in-person learning. Professional communities that require face-to-face collaboration as a membership condition. The need for spaces where you know everyone present is human, where the connection is unmediated and unoptimized, will become a kind of luxury.

> Will it matter to you that this is made by a machine? If it does, and I think it will for many, then creative work becomes one of the nonjob tasks that humans do for their own sake. Being a human artist might matter more than ever, precisely because the art itself is no longer scarce.

> If we are on the Escalator, we need to redesign organizations, invest in training, and start to make deliberate choices about education and culture and governance. We need to consider what it means when AI is not used as a tool to be adopted but as a force that reshapes our world. The decisions we make are particularly consequential right now because we have no models of the future. That gives you tremendous agency to make a difference.

> And yet, this book was not written by AI. To be fair, I say that with less confidence than I would like. Not because the AI wrote more than I have let on, but because it became increasingly hard to separate where the intellectual work was entirely my own and where it was developed in conjunction with AI. This is not a confession. It is, I think, just part of what co-existence feels like from the inside. The boundary between “my idea” and “an idea I had while talking to an AI” turns out to be blurrier than the introduction promised.

> The one thing I am sure of after writing this book is that co-existence is not a state you arrive at. It is something we will need to negotiate with each other, and with the strange technology we have created.
