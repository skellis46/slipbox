# Gill - Al-Barakeh Wheat

_previous note:_ [[Gill - Going for Broke]]

> The name of her enterprise is *Al-Barakeh*, which in her words, roughly translates as *the abundance and prosperity that results from working and being an inseparable part of All–people and life*. And in my seventeen years of baking, I can hardly think of anyone with a clearer purpose, stronger integrity, or more calloused hands (she plants and harvests wheat, in addition to milling).

> She inspires me because Lama and *Al-Barakeh* embody my belief that bakers should not only feed people, but nourish them and the places we live. And she does it in a place–Jordan–which is the cradle of bread (archaeologists there found the world’s oldest loaf there, made 14,4000 years ago). She and I both agree that bread doesn’t *just* feed us–it leavens the stories which keep us together.

> Let me give it to you in numbers. These are numbers for this year: one ton of white flour– not grains–is sold for $194 here in Jordan, milled from grains imported from Romania. One ton of local grains, not yet milled, is sold for $592. Approximately triple the price.

> This reminds me of something someone I truly admire once told me, “We can’t have a cuisine unless we protect its ingredients.”

> GG: I’d love to know about the different varieties of grain, not just that you grow now, but any varieties that were grown in the past. Because the Fertile Crescent is a birthplace not just of grain cultivation, but of agriculture as well. In fact, the oldest loaf of bread ever found was discovered in Jordan: it is 14,400 years old. And, as some readers may know, the hard-durum wheat native to Jordan was the first wheat ever domesticated by humans.

> Another wheat is called Nabi Jamal, or camel's tooth. I think in the United States you call it Khorasan [or Kamut]. It’s been dated to be more than 10,000 years old. We grow golden emmer or white emmer. We're also growing one variety called Elephant Tooth. It used to be a very old variety from West Iraq.

> I was very, very confused–What do you mean by two kilos for the ants? He said, you'll never have *Al-Barakeh* if you don't make sure that the ants will get their share. When he started to show us how to throw the seeds and sow the wheat, he taught us a prayer that is part of our ancestors’ traditions in the Levantine area.

> And the prayer goes like this:
>
> O’ *Lord, feed us and feed through us..*
>
> *Feed the birds in the darkness of the night..*
>
> *Feed the crawlers of the earth…*
>
> *Feed the strong one, the weak one one, and the one lying sick..*
