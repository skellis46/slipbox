# Unger and Narayanan - Steering AI Beyond Automation

_previous note:_ [[Cory Doctorow - Pluralistic Supervised AI Isn't]]

> There is an [alternative view](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854): that AI is a technology (or class of technologies) that, like any technology, can develop in different directions. Total automation is one direction. But there are others, including directions that focus more on human augmentation, and increasing the collaborative and collective power of teams of humans and AI models working together.

> What we instead call “white box agents” are AI systems that are epistemically more legible to their human users, and explicitly reveal their intermediate processes (sources, assumptions, methods, uncertainties, process artifacts). White box agents could open the door to all kinds of more direct forms of human-AI collaborations: if humans can see the intermediate processes, they can directly interact with them.

> A basic dynamic of active human collaboration is low latency. Imagine two people having a real conversation. If one person has to leave the room to think for 20 minutes every time you ask him a question, you’re no longer having a conversation; at best maybe you’re delegating to him. Models that can work at a speed that more naturally resembles human interaction might [better enable](https://cursor.com/blog/composer) forms of deeper human-AI collaboration.

> Low latency is particularly promising because it could enable workers to stay in a state of flow. Flow is a [concept from psychology](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/csikszentmihalyi%20so%20rich.pdf) that describes people’s experience when performing activities such as rock climbing or chess that people are drawn to for intrinsic reasons. It is a state of altered experience in which the performer forgets time and fatigue, and is immersed in the moment. Most programmers are [familiar with flow](https://github.blog/news-insights/research/research-quantifying-github-copilots-impact-on-developer-productivity-and-happiness/), and it is a big reason they are drawn to programming.

> The basic model of human-AI interaction, under the automation paradigm, is delegation: I tell my agent what to do, walk away, then come back later to verify. Many versions of “human in the loop” still come back to this; the human is [technically in the loop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23642), but just standing at the starting line and then waiting at the finish line. This is quite different from a model of collaboration, where the human is actively participating in the process, and continually steering the agent as it works.

> [Most AI benchmarks today](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12355) test how good a model is at completing a task on its own, not on how well a model collaborates with a human. (This is likely part of the reason for the gap between benchmark performance and real-world usefulness).

> Evaluating the [joint efforts of AI-human teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09833) is hard. This is a relatively simple challenge to articulate, but non-trivial to make progress on. It is more difficult to understand how to best evaluate AI-human collaboration, and it raises a complex set of questions that at first blush might seem uncomfortably subjective or confusing. How much disagreement between the AI model and the human is appropriate? How deferential should the model be, and how much control should the human have? How much space does a productive collaboration have for each party to learn from the other? How do we think about short term vs long term success of a team? What are the most objective ways to evaluate collaborative success, and what are the best signals and proxies to pick out of the data?

_reference:_ Gabriel Unger and Arvind Narayanan, AI as Normal Technology. "Steering AI Beyond Automation." https://www.normaltech.ai/p/steering-ai-beyond-automation
