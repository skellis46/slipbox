# Jacobs - Up From Darkness

_previous note:_ [[Alan Jacobs - A Humanism of the Abyss]]

> Humanism is a regulative ideal rooted in the conviction that human beings—whether defined as members of the species *Homo sapiens sapiens*, or as creatures made in the image of God, or in any other way—have certain obligations to one another as human beings that should govern, or at least shape, our social and political lifeworld. (This is not an *exclusive* statement about our obligations; in the realm of ethics, it is not sufficient—we owe debts to the nonhuman world as well—but it is necessary.)

> For Frans de Waal, in his brilliant book *Are We Smart Enough to Know How Smart Animals Are?*, a humanism that fully recognized and embraced all human beings would not be truly universal because it would be *anthropocentric*: “True empathy is not self-focused but other-oriented,” and, for de Waal, a humanism in which the white cis-het male genuinely and completely recognized the humanity of the nonbinary person of color would not be much of a victory. It would still be “self-focused,” species being a kind of self, on this account. “Instead of making humanity the measure of all things, we need to evaluate other species by what *they* are.”7

> My argument, more suggestive than definitive, is that whether humanism *should* be a functioning category for the ordering of our lifeworld, it always has been and always will be. The key challenge is to articulate and defend it in a useful and constructive way. The Catholic priest and philosopher Ivan Illich contrasted two kinds of technology: the *manipulatory* and the *convivial*. I contend that the concept of humanism is a kind of technology—it does work in the world—and that defining it in convivial rather than manipulatory ways is essential to its renewal.

> Morson takes some time to reflect on Dostoevsky’s theme, and he comes to what may be a surprising conclusion: “The human capacity that enables people but not tigers to enjoy torture is, oddly enough, *empathy*, which we usually think of as entirely positive. But empathy has a dark side; every con man relies on it.” (Emphasis mine.) “No one tortures a stone: The point of torture is to inflict pain that one can sense vicariously, while stripping not just a body but a soul until reaching its ultimate nakedness.”

> As Simone Weil argued in her great essay on the *Iliad*, “To define force—it is that *x* that turns anybody who is subjected to it into a *thing*. Exercised to the limit, it turns man into a thing in the most literal sense: It makes a corpse out of him.”16 A human being who is killed thereby ceases to be human. But only the human can be dehumanized.

> Imagine no humans had been present when David Wayne DePape broke into the Pelosi home, but a family dog had been. If DePape had assaulted a dog with his hammer, it is unlikely that he would have been celebrated or that the dog’s injuries would have been a topic of social-media amusement.

> The principle at work here is a simple one: Those human beings who have caused suffering to the innocent, or have been complicit in such suffering, deserve to suffer in return. In the modern understanding, an animal who injures or kills a person or another animal may be confined or executed for its actions, but this is done not to inflict reciprocal suffering—“an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth”—but strictly as a measure to prevent further actions of the same kind. Even those who think such measures are necessary rarely take *pleasure* in them.17 What I have called dark humanism arises from this distinctive version of human exceptionalism. It is indeed, I would argue, where humanism begins. But humanism doesn’t have to end there.

> But if Simone Weil is right, humans simultaneously share in the suffering of the nonhuman world and also experience something unique to us, which she calls *malheur*. This word, usually translated as “affliction,” refers to a condition that arises when one suffers *and*knows that one suffers *and* is aware of the causes of one’s suffering. Reflection on pain intensifies the pain and alters its existential character.

> This, I believe, is an object lesson with a universal application: Humanism can become something more than dark humanism only at the cost of sitting-with and staying-for. It is a retail business and cannot be done wholesale. If you want to condemn Paul Pelosi or Erika Kirk for marrying wicked people, well, nothing could be easier: Type a few characters, send them into the Infinite Online, and you’re done. Dark humanism can be achieved instantly. As Job almost said, Man is born to righteous indignation as the sparks fly upward.

> In his recent and fascinating book, *Language Machines*, Leif Weatherby questions “how two systems of representation—language and computation—interact” and how LLMs alter our conventional “distinction between culture and cognition.” To my purposes here, though, is an essential concept that he calls “remainder humanism.”24

> Weatherby issues a similar warning against the danger of a humanism of the gaps:
>
> > Ever since the celebrated AI critique of Hubert Dreyfus, who used Martin Heidegger’s notion of “being-in-the-world” to argue that computers were not, as humans are, “in-a-situation,” the guiding thread of AI critique has been, as Dreyfus’s title announced, “what computers can’t do.” The problem with this line is that it establishes a *moving* yet allegedly bright line between human and machine. Obsessively locating and relocating those differences is methodological Whac-A-Mole….26

> Take all that can linguistically and conceptually be done, subtract from that all that AI can do, and the *remainder* is the realm of the human. Weatherby uses this term in another way as well: Remainder humanism is “a humanism without a theory or doctrine of what is human, in which humanity is remaindered, like a book past salability.”27

> The logic employed in these critiques is that of *unique capabilities*, and so powerful is this logic that I have ended up verging on it at several points in this essay—though I have attempted to frame matters primarily in terms of *characteristic traits* and *horizons of possibility*. For Weatherby, by contrast, the lack of “a theory or doctrine of what is human” must be remedied by a more adequate account of *language*, specifically as language relates to computation.
