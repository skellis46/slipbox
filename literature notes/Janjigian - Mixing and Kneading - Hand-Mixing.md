# Janjigian - Mixing and Kneading - Hand-Mixing

_previous note:_ [[Janjigian - Know Your Dough]]

> **Hold back the salt, even without an autolyse.** The salt is always held back from a dough when doing an autolyse. If you add the salt with the flour, it will glom onto the water first, which can make it hard for the flour to hydrate evenly. This is true whether or not there is an autolyse: Merely waiting to add the salt until the flour and liquids are fully combined can make hand-mixing faster and less frustrating, since it minimizes clumpiness.

> **Dry into wet, not wet into dry.** In other words, *place liquid ingredients in the bowl first, then mix the flour and other dry ingredients into it, ideally a portion at a time.* When you combine large amount of liquid with a small amount of flour, it easily forms a smooth batter. Meanwhile, if you combine a large amount of flour with a small amount of liquid, it initially produces a clumpy, uneven mess. Even once you’ve added enough liquid to make a dough, it can take a lot of effort to get the clumpiness to go away.

> **Stiff into less-stiff.** If your dough includes wet-but-stiffer components—like stiff preferments, flour scalds, and porridges—combine those evenly with the remaining liquid first, using a whisk or dough whisk. That way they will get easily and uniformly distributed through the dough. Sometimes this isn’t possible to do. In some recipes, preferments get held back when the process includes an autolyse. Ditto for scalds or porridges that get added to a dough somewhat later in the mix. The hassle of incorporating these sorts of components into a dough after the initial mix by hand is one reason I avoid doing so whenever possible (or why I use a machine instead).
