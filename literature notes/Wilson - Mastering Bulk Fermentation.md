# Wilson - Mastering Bulk Fermentation

_previous note:_ [[Wilson - Open Crumb Mastery]]

> And just to be extra clear, bulk fermentation refers to the stage of fermentation that takes place prior to dividing the dough into individual loaves (or going straight into preshaping/shaping if you only have a one-loaf batch, as is common among home bakers). That's why we call it "bulk" – the dough has yet to be divided into separate loaves; it has yet to be individuated.

> So to sum it up, underfermented dough can be recognized by its lack of rise, lack of alveoli, and lack of flavor and aroma. Underfermented dough feels heavy and dense in the hand. It can't hold a shape – even after folding – so it spreads flat rather than standing tall. In the container, the dough does not exhibit the doming you'd expect from a well-risen dough, and if the container is clear you will not see much in the way of appreciable gas accumulation or discernable alveoli. And underfermented dough often has little to no taste of fermentation, nor any noticeable aroma.

> overfermentation is not typically prevalent during bulk. Since bulk only represents a portion of total fermentation time (though an admittedly large portion), it tends to have already concluded by the time overfermentation sets in. More commonly, overfermentation rears its ugly head during the final proof

> overfermentation – of which the telltale symptom is gluten degradation – is primarily caused by excessive bacterial/enzymatic activity. That is to say, it's caused more so from the process of lactic acid fermentation (bacterial) than alcoholic fermentation (yeast).

> The very first sign that your dough is beginning to turn proteolytic is that it starts to become stickier than it was before. What may have once been a smooth and sleek dough is now turning into a sticky and difficult to handle mess; leaving bits here and there stuck to anything it touches. It still holds a solid structure at this point, but the stickiness hints at worse things yet to come.

> Overfermentation = Gluten Degradation = Loss of Structure Overproofed = Excessive Gas = Incorrect or Damaged Structure

> The aliquot is only a proxy for the dough that is bulking, it is not the batch of dough itself. In essence, you now have two batches of dough – one larger and one smaller – and you are attempting to judge the readiness of the larger batch by measuring the smaller. When attempting to measure a thing, is it not best to measure the actual thing itself?

> The strength, structure and tension created through folding actually slow the rate of rise in a batch of dough because additional expansive force (generated by additional fermentation) is required to overcome the compressive force created by the folds.
