# Walker - AI vs Craft

_previous note:_ [[Ingold - Making]]

> This article is about creativity and AI through the lens of craft and materiality, by way of a [workshop](https://substack.com/redirect/1eb75443-fffc-4793-b7ef-8c3195eeec69?j=eyJ1IjoiYXZ6eDQifQ.G0OEO2hYU5EfmDn6Y1N-lMJfqyCMC6azYH_trtWPtnc) I was involved in recently, organized by Caterina Moruzzi (I profiled her [here](https://substack.com/redirect/3f84ddab-f420-419f-a9d0-88f3f6343c4f?j=eyJ1IjoiYXZ6eDQifQ.G0OEO2hYU5EfmDn6Y1N-lMJfqyCMC6azYH_trtWPtnc)) and Despina Papadopoulos as as part of a book they are co-authoring: *Material Encounters: Creativity in the Times of AI*. The idea was to engage with physical materials and a diverse range of practitioners. The goal was to explore how creativity works and might be defined. And the method was making: each practitioner brought tools and materials for a hands-on exchange during the second day.

> Yet all these craft traditions, these tools for thinking, are qualitatively different from AI: many people believe it’s designed to do the thinking for us. Papadopoulos and I, however, have been treating it as a *thing to think with* (to borrow Seymour Papert’s phrase). Papadopoulos’ concept of *philosophy machines* means exactly that, and to me the most important thing about AI isn’t what it does but how it prompts us to think about concepts like artificiality, intelligence, and creativity.

> Papadopoulos and Moruzzi had kicked off the workshop with an already-formed working definition as a prompt: Creativity is “the process of expressing intentionality through engagement with the material of one’s domain.” Everyone responded in different ways to this.

> Bichakis added, “I remember the talk of an entomologist—he said it is important work to name things. Because then you can protect them. It’s an important aspect of caring about them.”

> Bichakis suggested shifting the discussion. “We’re back to the idea that creativity is a verb, not a fixed point – and verbs aren’t as easy to find the opposite of.”

> If you think AI has nothing to do with material reality, researcher Kate Crawford will set you straight. “This is the basic material cycle of AI’s metabolism: consumption and excretion patterns where AI-generated images have material consequences and consequences for our materials.” [[Source](https://substack.com/redirect/71ac47ef-773f-4e50-a0d5-0ebc632afea6?j=eyJ1IjoiYXZ6eDQifQ.G0OEO2hYU5EfmDn6Y1N-lMJfqyCMC6azYH_trtWPtnc)] She goes into depth about the environmental impacts in her book *Atlas of AI*.

> “AI is very good at starting a project,” said Adamopoulos, “but it’s very bad at actually seeing it through and completing it. A lot of AI-assisted projects end up half-baked, because finishing requires something else, a kind of spirit.”

> “For me,” said Pappas, “it’s a very true, existential dread. It’s so exciting to open up the possibilities and start a project. But when you start narrowing down and making decisions, there’s a certain feeling of grief. You’re saying no to all of the other thousand things.”
